True Semantics: Does AI Really Need Embodiment?

February 17, 2026

A child touches an apple, smells it, tastes it, drops it, watches it fall, and finally hears the crunch when they bite it. Only later does the word apple connect to that experience. Instead of memorizing a symbol, the child builds a multisensory model.

Language vs. Lived Experience

A large language model, by contrast, learns the token “apple” from text. It sees sentences such as “An apple is a fruit,” “She baked an apple pie,” or “Apple released a new iPhone.”

From those examples, it builds statistical relationships between words and meanings. This raises a deeper question: can AI reach true semantic understanding through language alone, or does it need embodiment?

The Human Model of Meaning

Human meaning is grounded in experience. When people think of an apple, they may connect it to shape, color, texture, smell, and taste. Meaning is tied to physical reality and direct interaction with the world.

The AI Model of Meaning

Large language models do not physically experience the world. Instead, they learn meaning from patterns in data. Words that appear together often become connected in the model’s internal structure.

Even so, language carries traces of human experience. Because people describe the world through language, models can still capture useful patterns of meaning.

The Main Question

The central issue is whether symbols can truly mean something without direct grounding in the world. Some argue that embodiment is necessary. Others believe rich language and multimodal systems may be enough to approximate understanding.

Final Thought

The debate over embodiment is both technical and philosophical. It pushes us to think more carefully about what understanding really means, for humans and for AI.
